## Introduction
Centripetal force, is 'center-seeking force'. 

Like mechanics, there's also formulas for this, but luckily, there's only two:
$$a_{c}=\frac{v^{2}}{r}$$
$$F_{c}=\frac{mv^{2}}{r}$$
The first formula is the centripetal *acceleration*, and the second is the centripetal *force*. Both are center-seeking.

$m$ is the mass.\
$r$ is the radius of the circle.\
$a_{c}$ is the centripetal acceleration.\
$F_{c}$ is the centripetal force.

## Applications:
For centripetal force and acceleration, there isn't much calculation to do. The calculations are often achieved-level. If you can perform kinematic calculations, you can 100% perform centripetal force/acceleration calculations. However the explaining part often goes up to M/E level. 

Most common non-calculation question they ask is "Add an arrow to show the direction of acceleration", and "Add an arrow to show the direction of velocity".

The first one -- drawing arrow for acceleration, draw the arrow TOWARDS THE CENTER OF THE CIRCLE. Centripetal acceleration is always pointing towards the inside of the circle.

Whereas the second one -- drawing arrow for velocity, draw the arrow AT THE TANGENT TO THE CIRCLE (so that it is perpendicular to the centripetal acceleration). 

Another common question is "Explain how {something} can be having constant speed, but not constant velocity/acceleration".

The answer to this is back to the physics principle of how velocity and acceleration is a *vector quantity* whereas speed is a *scalar quantity*. Vector quantities MUST have direction in them. The direction at which {something} is rotating in a circle always changes (since it's a tangent, and it always changes), same with acceleration; hence the velocity and acceleration constantly changes because its direction is also constantly changing! On the other hand, speed is a scalar quantity that doesn't have a direction. If it's just moving at the same speed constantly, nothing changes, so it's speed is constant. There's no need to include in directions in speed.
